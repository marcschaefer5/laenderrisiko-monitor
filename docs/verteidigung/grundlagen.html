The basics · six steps

From one news report to one warning.

Nothing on this page requires prior knowledge. It follows a single real event — recorded on 1 February 2025 and used in this thesis — through every step that turns it into a number, and finally into a warning. Six steps, one scroll.

Scroll to begin

Step 00 · the data source

A machine reads the news and writes down what happened.

GDELT is a public research project. Every fifteen minutes it scans news reports from around the world and, for each event it recognises, writes one row into a table: who did something, to whom, where, how severe it was, how widely it was reported.

No person fills in those rows. A program infers them from the article text. That is the raw material of this thesis — not anyone's opinion about risk, but a count of reported events.

And that is the catch: the machine never checks whether it understood the article correctly.

Step 02 · from many to one

The model never sees a single event.

One event says nothing about a country's risk. What matters is the pattern: how much is being reported today compared with a normal day, how negative it reads, how severe it is on average.

So every event for one country on one day is counted, averaged and summed into a single line. Over one year and two countries, 6.2 million events become 730 lines.

From here on, a day is one row. Individual reports no longer exist.

Step 03 · what a day looks like

Seven numbers describe a country's day.

This is the whole input. No headlines, no text, no context — seven figures per country and day, each one a count, an average or a difference computed from the events of that day.

The first one, the smoothed volume of reporting, turns out to be the strongest predictor of all — stronger than any measure of violence.

Step 04 · unusual, not large

There are 27 American events for every German one.

Raw counts cannot be compared between the two countries. So each day is expressed as a distance from that country's own recent normal — the average of its last 60 days, measured in standard deviations.

A quiet German day and a quiet American day then look alike. And a day counts as a warning day when the series rises more than 1.5 standard deviations above that normal within the next three days.

That crossing is what the model is trained to anticipate.

The same picture works for either country

Step 05 · the decision

The model returns a probability. A human draws the line.

For every day the model outputs a number between 0 and 1. It never says „warning“ by itself — someone has to decide how high that number must be before anyone is told.

Both thresholds were picked by the same rule, and the rule produced two different answers: Germany warns early and often, the USA warns rarely and only when it is fairly sure.

Where the line sits is a judgement about the cost of a false alarm.

And then the honest part

Those five steps describe how a warning is produced. They say nothing about whether the warning is any good. Answering that needs two more things the thesis did not have: a benchmark to beat, and a target that does not come from the same data. The rebuild after submission supplied both — and the answer changed.
